# Homework 3 — Cleaning and exploring messy data

## Overview

In HW3 you go through the whole cycle on a dataset where the cleaning decides the answer. The
focus is the week 3 material: **missing values** and why they are missing, **odd categories and
outliers**, **checks in code**, and comparing groups **honestly**, with uncertainty and with an
eye on a third variable that could explain the difference.

The data are given, and so is a broad question. Making the question precise is part of the work:
which groups you compare, and who you include. Write down every choice and why. A reviewer should
be able to disagree with a decision and still follow your report.

Read [week 3](/lectures/week-3) first. It works through the same steps on another dataset.

## Learning goals

- Turn a broad question into one the data can answer.
- Count missing values, find out who is missing, and say what your way of handling them does to
  the answer.
- Write checks in code, and a data dictionary.
- Compare the rate of an event between groups, with a 95% interval and a permutation test.
- Recognise a confounder, and compare like with like.
- Write conclusions that state their uncertainty and their limits, without claiming cause.

## The question

**Is stroke more common among smokers, and does age explain it?**

This is your starting point; refine it. For example: which smoking groups do you compare (people
who smoke now, who smoked before, who never smoked)? Who do you include (all ages, or only
adults)? What does “more common” mean: a difference in the share who had a stroke, or a ratio?
State your refined question at the start of the report, and say why you chose it.

## The data

[stroke-data.csv](/data/hw3/stroke-data.csv) (317 KB), 5 110 rows: one row per person, with
their age, some health information, their smoking status, and whether they had a stroke. Only
249 of them did.

**Where it comes from.** The file was published on Kaggle as the
[Stroke Prediction Dataset](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/fedesoriano/stroke-prediction-dataset)
by the user fedesoriano in January 2021, and copied to the course's
[data repository](https://github.com/su-mt4008/data). Kaggle lists the licence as “Data files ©
Original Authors”, and the description says only “(Confidential Source) - Use only for
educational purposes”. So we don't know who these people are, in which country, when they were
measured, or how they were chosen. That matters for what you can conclude, and is part of the
assignment.

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `id` | a number for each person |
| `gender` | `Male`, `Female` or `Other` |
| `age` | age in years |
| `hypertension`, `heart_disease` | 1 if the person has high blood pressure / a heart disease, else 0 |
| `ever_married` | `Yes` or `No` |
| `work_type` | `children`, `Govt_job`, `Never_worked`, `Private` or `Self-employed` |
| `Residence_type` | `Urban` or `Rural` |
| `avg_glucose_level` | average blood sugar; the unit is not stated |
| `bmi` | body mass index, kg/m²; `N/A` when missing |
| `smoking_status` | `never smoked`, `formerly smoked`, `smokes`, or `Unknown`: “the information is unavailable” |
| `stroke` | 1 if the person had a stroke, else 0 |

Save the file **unchanged** in `hw3/data/raw/`, and fill in its row in `hw3/data/raw/README.md`.
Open it in a text editor first. Then check what your reading function did with `N/A`: pandas
reads it as missing by default, but R's `read_csv` reads the whole `bmi` column as text unless
you add `na = c("", "NA", "N/A")`.

## Tasks

Follow [How we work](/guide#hand-in-an-assignment): update `main`, and make a branch called
`hw3` from it. Then write `hw3/report.qmd`, one section at a time:

0. **Set up the folder.** Your name in the header of `hw3/report.qmd`, and the data’s row in
   `hw3/data/raw/README.md` (source, licence or terms, retrieval date).
1. **Question** — your refined question, and why a reader might care. Say what kind of answer
   data like these can give: the people were observed, not assigned to smoke.
2. **Data** — what one row is, and what is known and not known about where the data come from.
   What can it *not* tell you?
3. **Cleaning** — read the file and show that it was read correctly (shape, types). Then:
   - write **checks in code** (assertions) for what you expect: one row per `id`, only 0 and 1 in
     the 0/1 columns, ages and BMI values in a possible range, the categories you expect in each
     text column. If a check fails, find out why before you change anything;
   - **missing values**: count them per column you use, and count `Unknown` in `smoking_status`
     as missing too. Then find out *who* is missing: compare the ages of people with and without
     a known smoking status, and the share with a stroke among people with and without a BMI.
     Which kind of missing (MCAR, MAR, MNAR; see
     [why values are missing](/lectures/week-3#why-values-are-missing)) is most plausible, and
     what does your choice (drop, keep as its own group, …) do to your comparison?
   - **odd values**: the one person with `gender` `Other`, ages that are not whole numbers, the
     highest BMI values, and anything else you find. For each, decide whether it is an error or
     real ([outliers](/lectures/week-3#outliers-errors-or-real)), act on it in code, and say why;
   - a **data dictionary** for the columns you use, after cleaning
     ([example](/lectures/week-3#a-data-dictionary)).
4. **Exploration** — at least two figures, each with a caption saying what to see:
   - the share with a stroke in each smoking group, with the number of people in each;
   - how age differs between the smoking groups, and how the share with a stroke depends on age.
   Describe what you see in a few sentences.
5. **Analysis** — answer your refined question:
   - a table with one row per smoking group you compare: number of people, number of strokes,
     the share with a stroke, and a **95% interval** for that share. (A share is the mean of a
     0/1 column, so the formula from
     [statistics section 4](/statistics#_4-how-sure-are-you-standard-errors-and-confidence-intervals)
     applies, with $s^2 \approx \hat p(1 - \hat p)$; or use the bootstrap.)
   - the **difference** in the share between the groups you compare, with a bootstrap interval,
     and a **permutation test**: could a difference this large come from chance alone
     ([statistics section 5](/statistics#the-same-idea-by-computer-the-permutation-test))? Set a
     seed.
   - **compare like with like**: split the people into age groups, chosen before you look at the
     stroke shares within them (say why you chose them), and compare the smoking groups within
     each age group. Show the number of people and strokes in every cell. Does the difference
     you found remain?
6. **Conclusions & limitations** — answer your question in a few sentences, with the numbers and
   their uncertainty. Is age a **confounder** here
   ([statistics section 7](/statistics#_7-correlation-is-not-causation)), and what does that mean
   for the first comparison? Name what else could explain your result, and the limits of the
   data. If you tried more than one comparison, say how many
   ([statistics section 8](/statistics#_8-traps)).
7. **How to reproduce** — the commands that render your report.

Every number in your text should come from the code (inline code, see
[week 2](/lectures/week-2#tables-and-numbers-in-a-report)). Every figure follows the
[checklist for honest plots](/lectures/week-2#honest-plots).

Render with `uv run quarto render hw3/report.qmd` (Python) or `quarto render hw3/report.qmd`
(R), read `hw3/report.md`, and commit everything in `hw3/`. Change nothing outside `hw3/`, except
the lockfile if you added a package.

## Hand in

1. Push your branch `hw3` and open a pull request **from `hw3` into `main`** titled **`HW3`**,
   by the deadline in the [schedule](/homework/#schedule). Opening the pull request is the
   hand-in. ([How](/guide#hand-in-an-assignment))
2. In the pull request description, write your refined question and your answer in two or three
   lines, and list the decisions a reviewer should look at (who you included, how you handled
   missing values and `Unknown`, your age groups).
3. Make sure the check is green. If it is red, see [When the check is red](/guide#when-the-check-is-red).
4. Don’t merge it yourself; we merge it when it is accepted.

Pushing straight to `main` is not a hand-in: only the pull request counts.

## After the review

Your own pull request gets a review by Thursday. You can push improvements until **Friday 23:59**;
we grade the pull request as it stands then.

## Peer review

After the deadline you get access to a classmate’s repository (until Friday morning). Review their
pull request by Thursday 23:59, as described in [Review a pull request](/guide#review-a-pull-request).
Comment on specific lines in **Files changed**, and see
[Reviewing a pull request well](/lectures/week-2#reviewing-a-pull-request-well). For HW3, check
especially:

- Is the refined question precise, and can these data answer it?
- Are missing values and `Unknown` counted and explained, and is it said what the chosen
  handling does? Would you have chosen differently, and would it change the answer?
- Are there checks in code, and a data dictionary? Are the odd values dealt with, with a reason?
- Does every share and difference come with the number of people and an interval? Is the seed
  set?
- Is age dealt with, and were the age groups chosen for a stated reason?
- Do the conclusions avoid claiming that smoking causes, or doesn't cause, stroke?
- One highlight and one concrete suggestion, pointing to a line or a figure.

## Scoring

HW3 is scored 0–100, and you need 65 to pass. Late hand-ins and the final deadline: see
[Assignments](/homework/). We look for:

- all seven sections, each doing its job, and a green check;
- a refined question, with a reason;
- missing values, `Unknown` and odd values handled in code, with reasons and with what they do to
  the answer; checks in code and a data dictionary;
- shares and differences with uncertainty, a permutation test, and a comparison within age
  groups;
- conclusions that match the evidence and its uncertainty, and real limitations;
- a specific, constructive peer review.
